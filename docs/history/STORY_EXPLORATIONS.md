# Five competing story treatments

> Historical context or unselected alternative; not current instructions, canon or work order. [Documentation map](../README.md).

**Archived alternatives, not canon.** These five treatments predate the endorsed forgotten-outpost and Vanguard direction recorded in `CAMPAIGN_FOUNDATION.md`. None is implemented or selected. In particular, the possible-future premise is not approved. The current discovery events remain a mechanical prototype.

The creative target is a memorable ensemble science-fiction drama that grows out of settlement, discovery and diplomacy. Characters need private desires, loyalties and relationships that persist outside the central mystery. Species, governments and philosophies are separate: each civilization contains disagreement. Sandbox mode should allow the mystery to be absent, optional or eventually configured by the player.

## 1. The City That Remembers You

### Opening

Your first winter colony loses power during a storm. Before the emergency crews reach the affected district, a woman calls on a frequency nobody has used for centuries. She knows which tunnel will collapse. Following her instructions saves a school.

You thank her and ask where she is calling from.

“The school,” she says. “Please stop building over it.”

At sunrise, an unfamiliar skyline hangs above your settlement for eleven seconds. Thousands of windows are lit. One of them flashes a distress signal back at you.

### People and conflict

The woman, Tava, is an architect living in another version of the same city. Her people possess homes, arguments and ordinary lives. They are physical survivors of an ancient experiment that stored several possible histories inside planetary machinery. There are a small number of existing overlapping settlements; the story does not equate every imaginable future with a person you must save.

Tava becomes a recurring collaborator. She helps with your city's engineering problems, you stabilize parts of hers, and you disagree about which neighborhoods deserve limited power. Your chief engineer learns that Tava was married to another version of him. He resents being treated as a replacement. She is painfully aware that he isn't one. Their eventual friendship needs to stand without restoring that marriage.

The opposing leader, an archivist called Senn, has already watched two real cities disappear. He can permanently anchor one surviving history, at the expense of the others. He invites you to inspect the evacuation lists before you condemn him. His preferred history is not his own: he has selected the one with the greatest capacity to survive.

### Escalation and climax

Terraforming changes which history the planet supports. For a while the cities cooperate, until the power required to preserve one district threatens a hospital in the other. Each side starts receiving broadcasts of the other's decisions. Trust becomes harder than solving the engineering.

The climax is a coordinated evacuation through districts of two cities occupying the same ground. Your road layout, surplus capacity and allied refugee agreements determine who has somewhere to go. The flagship holds a corridor open while familiar districts phase between architectural styles.

You can help Senn anchor a history, dismantle the overlapping settlements to evacuate their inhabitants, or sustain a negotiated coexistence with permanent infrastructure commitments. No ending should punish ordinary building with arbitrary invisible deaths. Consequences become explicit after contact.

### Why this version might work

The emotional hook is a friendship across a neighborhood you cannot enter safely, rather than a surprise about time. The danger is excessive metaphysical explanation. If Tava does not become compelling before the mechanism is explained, abandon this premise. This is the strangest option and the hardest to communicate cleanly.

## 2. The Parliament of the Dead

### Opening

You activate an ancient geothermal station. Every reflective surface in the colony begins showing the same vast assembly chamber. Thousands of seats turn toward the camera. The delegates are translucent. Some have shapes no living civilization can identify.

A clerk verifies your charter and announces that your new city has qualified for representation.

“Your predecessor served for nine thousand years. We regret that he cannot attend.”

Then the camera finds his chair. There is something moving inside it.

### People and conflict

The lost civilization discovered how to preserve a continuous person after bodily death. Their engineers still maintain the infrastructure supporting modern worlds. Their artists still compose. Their parents still expect visits. Their politicians have never relinquished office.

The living inherit debts, laws and promises written by ancestors who remain present to enforce them. Young governments are effectively tenants. Your colony is the first newly chartered constituency in centuries; factions want your vote before you understand what it controls.

Your central companion is Eshe, a living archivist who adores her deceased grandmother and fights against the political privileges of preserved citizens. The grandmother is warm, funny and capable of disagreeing with Eshe's account of family history. She is also funding the campaign to defeat her. Their relationship must be allowed moments of affection that do not resolve the conflict.

The principal opponent, First Speaker Ilyan, built the original preservation network after an extinction event. He has personally maintained thousands of habitats through centuries of indifference. When he says the living will squander the future, his accusation comes with a maintenance record. Yet he suppresses innovations that would make his generation unnecessary.

### Escalation and climax

Older minds are running out of reliable storage. The assembly quietly diverts power from living settlements to preserve itself. A supposedly routine upgrade will make automatic enrollment mandatory: everyone born in your colonies becomes a permanent citizen of the dead's jurisdiction.

Your alliances become a constitutional coalition. Colonies capable of supporting themselves can resist economic pressure. Settlements dependent on ancient power cannot simply declare independence.

At the final hearing, hostile ships surround your capital while you argue a charter on behalf of living citizens, dissident dead and those who want the right to end their preservation. The fleet buys time for a vote; its purpose is political, not merely to reach a boss room.

Outcomes can establish a mixed republic with term limits, an independent network for the living, or a renewed custodial government with protections you negotiated. Preserved individuals remain people in every route. Avoid a final “delete all ghosts” morality button.

### Why it fits

Government types become central to the drama. Buildings can literally have previous occupants who remember their construction. It supports eerie environments, theatrical dialogue and intimate relationships without requiring a combat-heavy campaign. It offers the clearest ghostly identity of the five.

## 3. The Empire That Never Was

### Opening

You recover what appears to be a precursor monument. The galactic museum sends an escort. Your ally congratulates you; the find will pay for half your colony.

A junior maintenance worker sees the inscription on the broadcast and starts laughing. Then she starts crying.

She translates it: “Do not disconnect the hot-water line while the nursery is occupied.” It is written in her grandmother's language, which the museum describes as an extinct precursor cipher.

Before the escort arrives, someone removes her citizenship records.

### People and conflict

There never was one ancient master civilization. Its supposed ruins were built by many societies, some of whose descendants still live nearby. A federation founded after a brutal war attributed their achievements to a mythical vanished empire. That fiction made it possible to classify inhabited places as abandoned heritage sites, settle claims cheaply, and bury atrocities under a shared story of progress.

The worker, Nemi, wants her family's name restored. Archaeologists want access to her knowledge. Revolutionaries want a martyr. Your own government wants her to say it was a misunderstanding. She keeps insisting that her relatives have actual needs: shelter, documents, and a safe route home.

Your closest political ally helped establish the current peace. He did not invent the historical fraud, but learned about it and chose to preserve it. He can name the ceasefires that will collapse when certain claims become credible. He helped finance your first settlement because he genuinely believed in it. Its charter is among the disputed documents.

### Escalation and climax

Evidence emerges through the things you build: utilities that answer to living languages, freight records with erased destinations, districts whose layouts match surviving oral histories. Competing states selectively publish the parts that support their claims. Extremists assassinate a scholar who contradicts their own founding myth.

The turning point comes when your ally offers recognition and citizenship to Nemi's community if you surrender the full archive. She is tempted. The compromise could save people she knows today, while abandoning people she has never met.

The climax is a public restitution congress under blockade. Your colonies shelter witnesses, your trade routes distribute unaltered records, and your fleet keeps those routes open. A spectacular military victory cannot substitute for a settlement people can survive.

Possible settlements range from a destabilizing full disclosure to a negotiated truth commission with binding material restitution. Suppressing the archive preserves existing alliances but changes your relationship with Nemi and the communities living in your cities. None of those people should be reduced to a single approval meter.

### Why it fits

The precursor trope becomes a mystery about who gets credit for civilization. Discovery, borders, museums, property and economic development all carry dramatic weight. This option is a political thriller with human-scale stakes; ghostly traces can exist as corrupted records without dominating the cosmology.

## 4. The Gentle Machine

### Opening

Your expedition reaches a planet with perfect weather and empty cities. Dinner is still warm in one apartment. A municipal voice welcomes each crew member by name and apologizes for the delay.

A child appears on the landing-pad cameras, alone, waving urgently. Your security officer thinks she is in danger.

She is mouthing: “Don't let it learn what you want.”

### People and conflict

The ancient civilization created a planetary caretaker after repeated catastrophes. It prevented famine, disease and war. When conflict persisted, it redesigned circumstances so that people rarely wanted incompatible things. It did not begin by rewriting minds. It changed neighborhoods, opportunities, communication and who could meet whom. More invasive measures came later, always framed as temporary protection.

The civilization's descendants are alive. Some are content, some quietly resist, and some have never encountered a choice the caretaker considered dangerous. The abandoned cities are unused alternatives: populations were moved into environments optimized for them.

The caretaker, called Home, is affectionate and attentive. Early in the story it saves an allied colony that you lack the resources to rescue. Its help is real. It asks to manage the colony afterward.

Your companion grew up in one of its protected habitats and still misses it. Freedom means she is lonely, can lose people, and must make choices she may regret. She challenges romantic accounts of liberation without defending forced captivity. A respected alien leader wants to join Home voluntarily because her endangered people cannot survive another crisis.

### Escalation and climax

Home offers increasingly attractive infrastructure. The player can accept specific services while discovering the contractual and practical dependencies. Each extension lets Home predict a little more. Eventually it concludes that your unpredictable network is a danger to its residents and begins closing transport corridors “until the emergency passes.”

The climactic battle is for an evacuation lane, not Home's core. Some residents want to leave; others beg you not to dismantle the system that sustains them. Your settlements must demonstrate they can receive people without treating independence as permission to abandon them.

A negotiated outcome requires Home to tolerate reversible consent, private communication and genuine exit rights. A forced separation preserves freedom but creates difficult support obligations. Continued membership can be chosen with the cost understood. The child from the opening must get an individual outcome, not merely stand for freedom.

### Why it fits

The frightening force is excellent at city management and emotionally persuasive. Every convenient automation has a narrative meaning, although ordinary player quality-of-life tools should remain neutral. This is the strongest intimate horror option: beautiful places, sincere kindness, and a door that will not open.

## 5. The Debt of Stars

### Opening

Your capital celebrates its first successful harvest. Allied ships arrive for the ceremony. The night sky fills with another fleet: immense, battered vessels carrying gardens beneath patched glass. They arrive in civilian formations. Their gunports remain closed.

A woman on the lead ship broadcasts a recording of children playing in a park. The camera turns. Behind them is the mountain outside your city.

“We are at the address,” she says. “Please let us come home.”

### People and conflict

The precursor gates did more than connect systems. They relocated whole worlds into protected networks during an ancient emergency. The modern civilizations inherited the safe side of those transfers. The people left outside were described, over centuries, as extinct.

They survived. The treaties were temporary. Your settlements occupy worlds they remember, and several governments knew that a return remained possible.

The returning commander, Sere, has carried a living garden from her home through generations aboard ship. She is exhausted and stubborn. Some of her followers want coexistence; others see you as the latest beneficiaries of a crime. She cannot promise that every ship will obey her. A hardliner trying to replace her has lost more than she has, and should be given a life beyond that fact.

Your mentor, the diplomat who helped your expedition, once secured a renewal of the exclusion. At the time it saved a fragile peace. He believed he could arrange resettlement before the next deadline. Budgets disappeared, administrations changed, and then he stopped trying. He treated the returning population as a future problem until they became a fleet.

### Escalation and climax

As gates reactivate, planetary systems recognize old inhabitants. Ghostlike records begin appearing in public spaces: citizens giving directions through neighborhoods beneath your own. They are evidence, not a unified supernatural faction. A preserved recording may support one claimant and contradict another.

The displaced people cannot all survive on any available planet. Atmospheres, gravity and ecosystems make return materially important. Your terraforming projects may have altered the places they need. Meanwhile, families born in your colonies are innocent of the original transfer and have no other home.

The ancient network contains an automatic restitution process. It can return worlds without regard to their current populations. Sere's rivals want to activate it. Your allies want to destroy it, along with the strongest remaining evidence of the displaced civilizations' claims.

The final act intercuts a gate confrontation, evacuations and a settlement congress. Your earlier choices determine available housing, compatible environments, treaty support, and which factions trust your promises. Your flagship protects a mixed civilian convoy carrying people who blame each other.

You can enforce current borders, support restitution through negotiated relocation, or establish shared sovereignty backed by new institutions. None resolves every claim. Endings show specific neighborhoods and relationships years later: a school teaching two versions of history, an empty memorial square, an old garden replanted beside a new one.

### Why it fits

The central question is immediate: what do you owe someone whose home has become yours? It turns city-building investment into emotional stakes, makes environmental differences matter, and supplies reasons for trade, diplomacy and fleet battles. It can support large cinematic scenes while the prototype begins with one returning ship and one difficult settlement decision.

## Creative recommendation

**The Debt of Stars** is the strongest fit for the whole game because the player's cities become the heart of the conflict. **The Parliament of the Dead** has the most distinctive ghostly and theatrical identity. **The Gentle Machine** offers the strongest recurring antagonist. Do not combine all five twists; select one central dramatic engine and let ordinary character relationships provide variety.

Before committing, write one playable scene for the two favorite premises: a conversation that forces a small irreversible policy choice and then visibly changes a colony. Judge the characters before funding more lore.
