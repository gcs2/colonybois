# The Vanguard and the worlds that forgot us

Current story treatment · 22 September 2026 · Frontier Worlds is a working title.

This document consolidates the chosen direction. Scenes and dramatic structure below are a proposed treatment of that direction, not a finished screenplay. Names, biological ancestry and the final resolution remain open. The gameplay consequences are in [Project vision](PROJECT_VISION.md).

## The premise

Your civilization was once somebody else's remote colonial outpost. Thousands of years ago, contact stopped. The colony endured. Emergency arrangements became institutions, institutions became nations, and people built a history of their own.

The ancestors became a disputed explanation for scattered foundations, incompatible machines and words whose meanings had been lost. Some believed they would return. Others believed there had never been anyone to return from.

But the founding civilization had survived the separation. It expanded, prospered, changed governments and eventually forgot you. Only much later did civil war and an invasion from outside its territory bring it close to extinction. That civilization was the United Nations of Earth.

You begin before your people know this. You will discover it while becoming responsible for colonies of your own.

The central question is personal and political: **once you can leave people behind, what will stop you becoming the ancestors?**

## A living city, not a surviving camp

The tutorial begins in an established city of roughly **120,000 residents**. It has apartments, offices, factories, civic institutions, busy streets, rail connections and access to space infrastructure. The original outpost belongs to its deep history. Contemporary people have invented things, prospered and fought over interests; they have not spent millennia waiting among wreckage.

The city should be satisfying to look at before the player touches anything: orderly blocks, a readable street hierarchy, dense neighborhoods, employment centers and a spaceport at the industrial edge. A preserved foundation in a park can suggest the past without turning every building into an archaeological prop. Latch and South Loop are provisional city and district names.

The target is SimCity 4-like urban fidelity. The population is represented through meaningful aggregate data and convincing scenery, not 120,000 individually simulated lives. Characters in the story are a small authored cast, not a promise to model every citizen.

This world already possesses spaceflight. The campaign is about gaining the authority, resources and relationships to lead a national interstellar program. It is not a compulsory march from primitive civilization to planetary unity.

The government is corrupt and inept. Its failure is visible in uneven services, protected contracts and neglected districts. It is still an institution with employees, supporters and functioning parts. Removing its leaders will not automatically replace everything it does.

## Opening chapter: the city learns your name

Proposed opening: a familiar morning commute passes through the city. Trains still run, shops open and launch vehicles stand beyond the freight yards. In one district, a preventable utility failure closes workshops and leaves homes without reliable heating or power. The administrative quarter receives priority service.

You begin with responsibility for a limited reconstruction area. The first problem is playable: inspect a network, reconnect a route, choose where scarce capacity goes, and watch the neighborhood recover. No long history lecture is necessary. The player first learns who lives here and what governing feels like.

Your response creates a political network. A public repair effort can bring neighborhood organizers and workers into a mutual-aid coalition. An institutional route can secure resources through engineers, civil servants and influential sponsors. Either can solve the immediate problem; each gives different people a claim on your future decisions.

A proposed escalation comes when the government tries to seize, obstruct or monopolize the successful reconstruction effort. The dispute becomes larger than a broken utility. Your coalition becomes the Vanguard.

The player can pursue an open rebellion or an institutional coup. This is the chosen political direction, but the exact confrontation remains to be written. In the first playable story slice it should be resolved through coalition support, city operations and consequential decisions, rather than requiring an additional ground-combat game.

Taking power grants authority over a nation, not the whole world. The most important scene follows the victory: the city still needs supplies tomorrow, neighboring governments want assurances, and the people who brought you to power expect you to remember your promises.

## Second chapter: a nation among neighbors

There are independent countries on the homeworld. They have conflicting interests, different institutions and their own ambitions in orbit. Some recognize your government; some bargain; some prefer the old regime. They are more than obstacles on a unification checklist.

The first diplomacy grows from familiar needs: transport access, water, energy, trade and launch infrastructure. A neighboring state may help finance a joint orbital program in exchange for shared command. Keeping the program national preserves discretion but costs more and narrows access.

The Vanguard is not automatically the permanent name of the state or a single fixed ideology. The way you took power influences your starting capabilities and obligations. Later constitutional choices can preserve, reform or betray the coalition that made you possible.

Space does not erase domestic rivalry. A rival can establish its own offworld settlement. Two nations can share a planet, and one nation can stretch across several worlds. Federation, cooperation, coexistence and later domination are different paths; reaching space requires none of them to end in planetary unity.

## Third chapter: the trail becomes inhabited

Your flagship enters nearby systems under a frontier fog of uncertainty. Worlds offer resources, strange life and places to settle. They also contain traces of a wider civilization: abandoned installations, engineering standards your machines partly recognize, familiar emblems used in unfamiliar ways.

The trail cannot consist mainly of recordings. It must intersect people who are alive and things the player can do. Proposed encounters include a settlement using an ancestral facility as essential infrastructure, traders whose contracts preserve an old jurisdiction, and an alien envoy whose society remembers a war your people have never heard of.

Evidence should force decisions. Excavating a site may displace a present community. Restoring a machine may make a harsh world livable while strengthening someone's territorial claim. Sharing a discovery may win partners and surrender an exclusive advantage. Leaving something untouched must sometimes be a useful choice.

Recurring characters carry these disagreements across chapters. Their roles can include an organizer from your first district, a rival homeworld negotiator, an offworld colony representative and an envoy connected to the former invading powers. Names, appearances and personal histories are still to be developed. Each needs interests beyond explaining lore to the player.

## Fourth chapter: Earth was real

The discoveries establish that the United Nations of Earth existed, that it founded the original settlement, and that it continued long after contact ended. Your people were not simply stranded by the final collapse. They were absent from a flourishing civilization's attention.

This revelation should change current politics. Some citizens may demand recognition as successors. Others will reject obligations imposed by a state that abandoned them. Neighbors may contest who has the right to claim the inheritance. The player must decide what an ancestral connection means in practice.

Earth's civilization was neither a perfect lost golden age nor a secret villain whose destruction needs celebrating. Its people built worthwhile things and committed serious wrongs. Its civil war must involve identifiable institutions, interests and decisions. The exact causes remain unwritten.

The outside invaders were not a species of uniformly evil people. Different leaders, factions and civilians can have different responsibility, motives and memories. We must still be able to name atrocities and hold their perpetrators responsible; complexity must not become an excuse for extermination. Descendants and present governments do not automatically share their ancestors' guilt.

Ghostly imagery can come from absence: an illuminated transit concourse serving nobody, an automated welcome addressed to a vanished administration, a maintained garden on an apparently deserted station. Literal ghosts, future echoes and a supernatural explanation are not established canon.

## The ending is ahead of us

The first story campaign should have a deliberately authored historical truth and a canonical resolution. **That resolution has not yet been selected.** We should not replace the unanswered ending with an arbitrary final boss or declare a preferred government the only valid outcome.

A promising dramatic destination is a present crisis in which the player can repeat or reject the pattern of abandonment. The exact threat, location and surviving actors remain proposals. It should bring the home city, dependent colonies and foreign relationships into the same decision, using capabilities earned during play.

The historical answer can remain consistent while the player's coalition, institutions, casualties, friendships and settlements differ. A canonical backbone must not erase those differences in the final cinematic. Reaching space is an expansion of the story; resolving its main conflict should permit continued strategic play.

## Marks and remembered history

The currency is **Marks**. Proposed history: early colony stores recorded claims on labor and materials as ledger marks. Municipal clearing houses later standardized those accounts, and modern banking retained the name. This is contemporary money with a historical origin, not a survival token that must remain redeemable for heat or food.

Other nations need not use Marks. The interface can show foreign settlements in the player's accounting equivalent without adding an exchange-rate simulator. Marks buy services and support budgets; physical supplies and construction materials remain separate resources.

## Replay and remaining decisions

The first campaign fixes its major facts and character motivations. Limited seeded variation can change opportunities and local conditions. Later story variants can choose different coherent histories at scenario creation; events must then respect that history and the player's actions. Randomness must not retroactively rewrite an ally's identity merely to manufacture a twist.

Still open: the player species and its biological relationship to humanity; why contact originally ended; the homeworld and national names; the causes and participants of Earth's civil war; the invading coalition; Earth's current condition; surviving ancestral societies; recurring characters; and the exact canonical ending. Humans and original alien species belong in the wider visual exploration, without settling these questions accidentally through a concept image.

The five treatments in [Story explorations](STORY_EXPLORATIONS.md) remain archived alternatives. They are not additional canon layered onto this story.
